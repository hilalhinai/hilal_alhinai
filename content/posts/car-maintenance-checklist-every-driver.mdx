Most cars don't break down because something suddenly fails. They break down because something small was ignored long enough to become something large. A £15 wiper blade becomes a motorway near-miss; an underinflated tyre becomes a blowout; a slightly low coolant level becomes a warped cylinder head.

The good news is that the checks which prevent almost all of this take a few minutes and require no tools. Here's the checklist worth actually following, grouped by how often each item needs your attention.

## The problem with "I'll get it serviced"

Servicing matters, but a garage sees your car twice a year at most. In between, you're the only person watching it — and the failures that strand you or cost you thousands usually announce themselves weeks in advance, in ways a driver can spot but a six-month service interval can't catch in time.

Treating the annual service as your entire maintenance strategy is like seeing a dentist twice a year and never brushing. The professional visit is necessary and insufficient. What fills the gap is a handful of habits that take less time than scraping ice off the windscreen.

## Common mistakes

**Waiting for a warning light.** Dashboard lights are a last line of defence, not an early one. By the time the oil-pressure light comes on, damage may already be happening. Many of the most important things to check — tyre wear, wiper condition, a slow coolant leak — have no light at all.

**Only checking when something feels wrong.** Cars are engineered to feel normal right up until they don't. A brake pad can be almost gone while the brakes still feel fine, because the warning is designed to arrive with a little margin, not a lot.

**Assuming new means maintenance-free.** Newer cars need less frequent attention, not none. Tyres still lose pressure, fluids still deplete, and a modern turbocharged engine is often less tolerant of missed oil changes than an older one, not more.

**Doing everything once, then nothing.** A burst of enthusiasm where you check all forty things in one afternoon achieves very little. Maintenance is a rhythm, not an event.

## The checklist

### Weekly (two minutes, no tools)

Walk around the car before you drive. You're looking for the obvious: a tyre that looks lower than the others, fluid on the ground where it was parked, a light that's out.

Check your tyres look evenly inflated and glance at the tread. Test that all your lights work — headlights, brake lights, indicators — which is easiest with a wall or a helper. Top up screen wash if the warning has appeared. Notice any new noise, smell or vibration; these are your earliest signals and they're free.

### Monthly (ten minutes)

Check tyre pressures properly with a gauge when the tyres are cold, against the figures on the sticker inside the driver's door — not the maximum printed on the tyre itself. Correct pressure is the single highest-return habit here: it affects safety, fuel economy and how long the tyre lasts.

Check your oil level with the dipstick on level ground, engine off and cool. Look at the coolant reservoir level against its min and max marks. Glance at the brake fluid reservoir. Test your wipers and replace them if they smear — they're cheap and they're a safety item the moment it rains.

### Every few months / seasonally

Look at your tyre tread depth honestly. The legal minimum in the UK is 1.6mm, but grip in the wet falls off well before that; many drivers replace around 3mm. Check that your spare (if you have one) is inflated, or that your tyre-repair kit is present and in date.

Before winter, check your battery's age — most last three to five years — and that your coolant is at the right concentration for the cold. Before summer, pay attention to your air conditioning and check coolant again, since heat is when cooling systems get tested.

### At every service

Let the garage handle the items that need ramps and expertise: brake pads and discs, the timing belt at its scheduled mileage, transmission fluid, suspension components, and a proper diagnostic scan. Your job isn't to do these — it's to make sure they actually happen on schedule rather than "whenever."

## The one check that matters most

If you only ever do a single thing from this list, make it tyre pressure.

It sounds almost too dull to be important, and it quietly affects more than anything else on the car. Correct pressure means shorter stopping distances, better grip in the wet, more even tyre wear, and measurably better fuel economy. Underinflation does the opposite on every count, and badly underinflated tyres run hot — which is how blowouts happen at speed.

The check takes ninety seconds with a £5 gauge, done when the tyres are cold, against the figures on the sticker inside the driver's door. Not the number moulded into the tyre sidewall — that's the tyre's maximum, not your car's recommended pressure. Do this monthly and before any long trip, and you've addressed the single highest-value maintenance item there is, for the price of a coffee.

## Action steps

1. **Do the weekly walk-around now,** before you read further. It takes two minutes and you'll likely find one thing.
2. **Find your door-sticker tyre pressures** and note them somewhere you'll see them.
3. **Buy a £5 tyre gauge** and keep it in the glove box.
4. **Set two recurring reminders:** a monthly fluid-and-pressure check, and a seasonal one before winter and summer.
5. **Write down your car's service schedule** by mileage so you know what's due when — the subject of [a fuller guide here](/blog/complete-car-maintenance-schedule-by-mileage).

## Keeping track of it

The reason maintenance lapses isn't laziness — it's that the information is scattered. The last oil change is on a receipt in a drawer, the service is a vague memory, the tyre pressures are on a sticker you never look at. When nothing is written down, "when did I last check this?" has no answer, so the check doesn't happen.

Keeping a simple record fixes most of it: dates, mileage, what was done, and what's due next. That can be a notebook in the glove box or a note on your phone.

## The bottom line

Cars are far more predictable than they feel. The failures that cost the most and hurt the most are, overwhelmingly, the ones that gave weeks of warning to anyone paying a few minutes of attention.

You don't need to become a mechanic. You need a two-minute weekly glance, a ten-minute monthly check, and a seasonal look before the weather turns.
