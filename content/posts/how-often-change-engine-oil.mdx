Ask five people how often to change your oil and you'll get five answers, most of them out of date. The "every 3,000 miles" rule is one of the stickiest pieces of car folklore there is — and for most modern cars, it's wrong by a wide margin.

Here's what actually determines the right interval, why the number rose so dramatically, and how to find the figure that fits your specific car and how you drive it.

## The short answer

For most modern cars, the correct interval is **every 7,500 to 10,000 miles, or every 12 months, whichever comes first** — and some manufacturers using fully synthetic oil specify 15,000 miles or more.

But the honest answer is that there's no universal number, because it depends on your engine, your oil, and how you drive. The single most reliable source is your owner's handbook, which states the manufacturer's interval for your exact car. That figure beats any rule of thumb, mine included.

## Why the 3,000-mile rule persists

It was genuinely correct once. Decades ago, engines were built to looser tolerances and oils broke down faster, so 3,000 miles was sensible. The number stuck for two reasons: people don't update inherited advice, and the businesses that profit from oil changes have no incentive to correct them.

Meanwhile, both engines and oils improved enormously. Modern synthetic oils resist heat and breakdown far better than the mineral oils of the 1970s, and engines are machined to tighter tolerances. The result is that following the 3,000-mile rule today mostly means changing perfectly good oil, wasting money and creating needless waste — not damaging anything, but not helping either.

## What actually determines the interval

**The oil type.** Fully synthetic oil lasts considerably longer than conventional mineral oil and tolerates more heat. Modern cars are usually specified for synthetic, and using it is part of why intervals have stretched. Always use the grade your handbook specifies — the numbers like 5W-30 aren't interchangeable.

**The engine.** Manufacturers design and test their engines and publish an interval for each. A turbocharged engine, which runs hotter and works the oil harder, often has a shorter interval than a naturally aspirated one. This is why "my friend's car goes 15,000 miles" tells you nothing about yours.

**How you drive.** This is the factor people miss. Counterintuitively, gentle short trips are harder on oil than long motorway runs. Which brings us to the most important distinction.

## Normal vs "severe" service

Most handbooks list two intervals: normal, and "severe" — and severe is far more common than the name suggests. Your driving counts as severe if it mostly involves:

Short trips under about ten miles, especially in cold weather. Stop-start city traffic. Lots of idling. Towing or heavy loads. Very hot, cold, or dusty conditions.

The reason short trips are hard on oil is that the engine never fully warms up. Condensation and fuel that get into the oil normally burn off at operating temperature; on short journeys they don't, so the oil degrades and contaminates faster despite low mileage. A car that only does a five-mile commute and the school run is a severe-service car, and should follow the shorter interval even though it covers few miles.

If that describes your driving, use the severe interval — typically the reason a low-mileage car still needs its annual oil change.

## Why the "12 months" part matters

Oil degrades with time as well as mileage. It absorbs moisture, its additives deplete, and it oxidises even sitting still. A car driven 4,000 miles a year hasn't reached a mileage-based interval, but after twelve months the oil should still be changed.

This is why the rule is always "miles or months, whichever comes first." Low-mileage drivers who wait to hit a mileage number can leave the same oil in for years, which is its own form of neglect.

## Common mistakes

**Following a generic number instead of the handbook.** Whether it's 3,000 from tradition or 10,000 from an article like this one, a general figure is a guess. Your handbook has the real answer for your car.

**Ignoring the time limit.** Low annual mileage doesn't mean skipping the yearly change. Time degrades oil regardless of use.

**Using the wrong oil grade.** Putting conventional oil in an engine designed for synthetic, or the wrong viscosity, undoes the reason the interval is long in the first place.

**Assuming the light knows.** Many cars have oil-life monitors, and they're good — but they estimate from driving data, and they only help if you don't reset or ignore them. They're a useful backstop, not a licence to stop paying attention.

## How to check your oil (properly)

Between changes, the level check is your early warning, and most people do it slightly wrong. The reliable method takes two minutes.

Park on level ground, because a slope skews the reading. Check with the engine off and cool — or if it's warm, wait ten minutes for the oil to drain back down. Pull the dipstick out, wipe it clean, push it fully back in, then pull it out again and read the level; the first pull is always smeared and unreliable. The oil should sit between the minimum and maximum marks.

While you're there, look at the oil itself. Fresh oil is amber and translucent; darkening is normal and not a problem on its own. But oil that's gritty, milky, or smells of petrol is telling you something — milkiness can indicate coolant getting in, a potentially serious sign worth investigating rather than topping up and forgetting.

If the level is consistently dropping between changes, the engine is either burning or leaking oil, and that's worth diagnosing early. Running an engine low on oil is one of the quickest ways to cause the expensive damage an oil change is meant to prevent.

## Action steps

1. **Open your handbook** and find the specified interval and oil grade for your car. This overrides everything else.
2. **Decide honestly whether your driving is "severe."** Short trips and city traffic usually qualify.
3. **Set whichever limit comes first** — the mileage, or twelve months — as a reminder.
4. **Note the oil grade** so you or your garage use the right one every time.
5. **Log each change** with the date and mileage, so you always know when the next is due.

## The bottom line

For most modern cars, 7,500 to 10,000 miles or twelve months is a reasonable rule — but the only number that's truly right is the one in your handbook, adjusted for whether your driving counts as severe. The 3,000-mile rule isn't dangerous; it's just expensive and outdated.

Get the interval right, use the specified oil, respect the twelve-month limit, and keep a record.
