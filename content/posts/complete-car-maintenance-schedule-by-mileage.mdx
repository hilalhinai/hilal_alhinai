Cars are maintained on a schedule for a reason: parts wear at predictable rates, and replacing them before they fail is far cheaper than after. The problem is that the schedule lives in a handbook nobody reads, written across scattered tables that are hard to hold in your head.

Here it is in one place — what needs attention at each mileage milestone, from the first oil change to the six-figure territory where the big items come due. There's a printable version at the end to keep in the glove box.

## How to use this

These are typical intervals. Your car's handbook is the final authority, because manufacturers vary and a turbocharged or heavily driven car may need shorter intervals. Treat this as the map, and your handbook as the exact coordinates.

A note on "severe" driving: if you mostly do short trips, city traffic, towing, or drive in extreme heat or cold, shift most intervals earlier — often to the shorter figure your handbook lists for severe service. Gentle short journeys are harder on a car than long steady ones.

## Every 5,000–10,000 miles (or every 12 months)

This is the routine tier — the checks that keep everything else healthy.

Change the engine oil and filter at the interval your handbook specifies (for most modern cars this falls in this range; see [the full oil-change guide](/blog/how-often-change-engine-oil)). Rotate the tyres to even out wear and extend their life. Inspect brakes, belts and hoses for wear. Top up and check all fluids — coolant, brake, power steering, washer. Check tyre tread and pressures properly.

Skipping this tier is what causes most of the expensive failures further down the list, so it's the one that matters most.

## Every 15,000–30,000 miles

The first tier of replaceable parts comes due.

Replace the engine air filter, which protects the engine from dust and affects both performance and economy. Replace the cabin air filter, which affects the air you breathe and your demisting. Inspect brake pads and discs closely — many cars need front pads somewhere in this range. Check the battery's condition and age. Inspect suspension components and steering.

## Every 30,000–40,000 miles

Fluids and fuel-system items enter the picture.

Replace the fuel filter on many cars. Inspect or replace spark plugs, depending on type — some modern long-life plugs last far longer, others are due around here. Inspect the transmission fluid. Give the brakes a thorough check, as pads and sometimes discs are commonly replaced in this window. Look closely at belts and hoses for perishing.

## Every 40,000–60,000 miles

The mid-life service, and the start of the bigger-ticket items.

Replace brake fluid, which absorbs moisture over time and loses effectiveness — often overlooked, and important for braking performance. Inspect or replace the coolant depending on type and age. Replace spark plugs if not already done. Inspect the transmission and, on some cars, change its fluid. Check the suspension and steering thoroughly.

This is also where tyres are frequently replaced, depending on how they've worn.

## Every 60,000–90,000 miles

The tier where neglect gets expensive, and where the single most important item lives.

**If your car has a timing belt (cam belt), this is typically when it must be replaced** — often between 60,000 and 100,000 miles, or by age, whichever comes first. This is the most important scheduled item on the entire list: a snapped timing belt can destroy an engine, turning a few-hundred-pound scheduled job into a several-thousand-pound catastrophe. Cars with timing *chains* rather than belts don't need this, but you must know which yours has. If you take one thing from this article, make it this.

Also in this window: major coolant replacement, transmission service, and a close inspection of the water pump (often replaced alongside the timing belt, since the labour overlaps).

## Every 90,000–100,000 miles and beyond

The long-life items finally come due.

Replace long-life spark plugs if fitted. Replace coolant if not recently done. Inspect the transmission carefully. Give the suspension a thorough review, as components like shock absorbers wear gradually and are easy to miss. Address anything deferred from earlier tiers.

Past 100,000 miles the schedule essentially repeats, with more attention to parts that are now well-used.

## Timing belt or chain: how to tell

Because the timing belt is the highest-stakes item on this list, knowing which type your engine has is genuinely important — and it's easy to find out.

A timing *belt* is a toothed rubber belt that needs replacing on schedule. A timing *chain* is a metal chain designed to last the engine's life, usually needing no scheduled replacement. Same job, very different maintenance.

The quickest way to know is to search your exact make, model, engine size and year alongside "timing belt or chain," or to check the handbook or ask any garage. Do it once and note the answer, because the consequences of guessing wrong are severe: assuming you have a maintenance-free chain when you actually have a belt is exactly how engines get destroyed at 80,000 miles. If it's a belt, write down the replacement interval and treat it as the one appointment you never miss.

## Common mistakes

**Tracking by time when the schedule is by mileage, or vice versa.** Both matter. A low-mileage car still ages; a high-mileage car still accumulates wear regardless of the calendar.

**Missing the timing belt.** It's the costliest oversight in car maintenance, and it's entirely preventable because it's on a known schedule. Not knowing whether your car has a belt or a chain is the dangerous state.

**Doing routine items but skipping the big scheduled ones.** Regular oil changes are excellent, but they don't cover you for a 70,000-mile timing belt. Both tiers matter.

**Losing the history.** Without a record of what was done when, you're guessing — and guessing usually means either paying for work twice or missing it entirely.
