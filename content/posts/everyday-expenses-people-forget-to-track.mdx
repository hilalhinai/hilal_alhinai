When someone tells me their budget "doesn't add up," the missing money is almost never in the big categories. Rent is memorable. Car payments arrive on a schedule. What escapes is the middle layer — too small to plan for, too frequent to ignore.

Here are the fifteen that come up most, with rough annual figures. Your numbers will differ. The point is the order of magnitude, which is usually a surprise.

## 1. Subscriptions you stopped using

The streaming service you kept "for one more month" fourteen months ago. The app that converted from a free trial. The cloud storage tier you upgraded during one busy week.

Three forgotten subscriptions at $10 each is **$360 a year**. Most people carry more than three. Check your card statement for the last three months and look for anything you can't immediately justify — the recurring charges are easy to spot once you're looking for them.

## 2. Bank and card fees

Monthly account maintenance, ATM withdrawals outside your network, foreign transaction fees, the occasional overdraft. Individually trivial, and none of them appear in anyone's mental budget.

**$120 to $400 a year**, and close to all of it is avoidable with an account change.

## 3. Delivery fees, service fees, and tips

The meal costs $18 and the total is $27. The gap is delivery, service charge, and tip — real money that gets remembered as "$18 for dinner."

Twice a week at $9 of overhead is **$936 a year**, spent entirely on the convenience layer rather than the food.

## 4. Coffee and small drinks

The famous one, and worth stating carefully: a daily $5 coffee is **$1,300 a year**. That doesn't make it wrong. It makes it a $1,300 decision, which deserves the same consideration you'd give any other purchase of that size.

## 5. Parking, tolls, and transit top-ups

Paid in coins and taps, recorded nowhere. **$300 to $800 a year** for most commuters.

## 6. Annual insurance and registration

Car insurance, home contents, road tax, professional memberships. These are not forgotten so much as un-anticipated — they arrive once a year and feel like emergencies.

Divide the annual total by twelve and set that aside monthly. It converts a crisis into a line item.

## 7. Gifts

Birthdays, weddings, new babies, holidays, the leaving present for a colleague. Almost nobody budgets for gifts, and almost everybody buys them.

**$400 to $1,200 a year**, concentrated in a few unlucky months.

## 8. Medical and dental costs not covered by insurance

Co-pays, prescriptions, the dental work insurance covers only partially, physiotherapy. Unpredictable in timing, entirely predictable in aggregate.

## 9. Home maintenance and replacements

The kettle dies. The tap drips. A light fitting fails. Individually small, and reliably several times a year.

**$300 to $900 annually** for renters; considerably more if you own.

## 10. Pet costs beyond food

Vaccinations, grooming, boarding when you travel, the unexpected vet visit. Pet food gets budgeted because it's bought weekly. The rest doesn't.

## 11. Work expenses you never claim

The lunch you bought because the meeting ran late. The taxi you didn't submit. The equipment you bought yourself rather than requesting.

## 12. App purchases and in-app upgrades

Small, frictionless, and charged to a card you set up years ago. The friction that normally makes you think about a purchase is deliberately absent.

## 13. Clothing replacements

Not shopping trips — replacements. Shoes wear out, work shirts fray. This spending doesn't feel discretionary, so it rarely gets counted as clothing.

## 14. Fees on things you already pay for

Ticket booking fees, card surcharges, "convenience" charges, express shipping. Each one is a small tax on a purchase you'd already decided to make.

## 15. Cash

The most under-tracked category of all. Money withdrawn from an ATM leaves your account as one line — "$100 ATM" — and then vanishes into a dozen purchases nobody records.

If your budget has a persistent unexplained gap, cash is usually where it went.

## The fastest way to find your own

You don't have to wait a month to get a first estimate. Pull the last ninety days of statements — bank and every card — and work backwards.

**Sort by amount, smallest first.** Everything under about $30 is the layer you're looking for. Scanning chronologically hides the pattern; scanning by size makes it obvious.

**Look for repeats.** Any merchant appearing more than four times in ninety days is a habit, not an occasion. Multiply the ninety-day total by four for the annual figure.

**Find every recurring charge.** Same merchant, same amount, monthly. These are your subscriptions, including the ones you've forgotten the name of.

**Total your ATM withdrawals.** That figure is your untracked cash spending, and for most people it's the largest single number in this exercise.

Twenty minutes of this usually surfaces two or three categories the person would have sworn were negligible.

## Why these fifteen in particular

They share a structure worth noticing. Each one is small enough to skip conscious evaluation, frequent enough to accumulate, and — critically — attached to something you actually wanted at the time.

That last part is why willpower-based approaches fail here. None of these purchases were mistakes in the moment. The delivery fee bought you an evening back. The parking bought you a shorter walk in the rain. Judged individually, most were reasonable, and it's only in aggregate that they become a decision worth revisiting.

## What this adds up to

Take the middle of each range and the total lands somewhere around **$4,000 to $6,000 a year** for a typical household. That's not a rounding error. For many people it's the entire difference between saving something and saving nothing.

The uncomfortable part is that none of these are extravagances. There's no single decision to regret, which is exactly why they're invisible — no individual purchase was big enough to remember.

## The fix is not what you'd expect

The instinct is to cut all fifteen. That rarely lasts, because most of them are buying something real: time, convenience, relationships, a functioning household.

The more durable fix is to make them visible. Once you know delivery overhead costs you $900 a year, you can decide whether it's worth it. Some people conclude it is. That's a legitimate answer — it's just now a decision rather than a default.

Visibility requires capture at the moment of spending. Reconstructing a month from memory produces the same blind spots that created the problem: you'll remember the coffee and forget the parking, remember the restaurant and forget the delivery fee.

## Try this for one month

Don't change any spending. Don't set targets. Just record everything, including cash, for four weeks.

The number at the end will be wrong in at least two categories you were confident about. That gap — between what you believed and what you did — is the most useful piece of financial information you'll get all year, and no budgeting method can give it to you second-hand.
